Part A

Incident response

Preserving evidence integrity, notifying stakeholders, and capturing volatile data in the correct order.

Chain of custody

Maintains evidence integrity by documenting every person who contacts, accesses, or modifies physical or digital evidence.

  • Hardware — Use tamper-evident sealed bags; log each transfer of custody with date, time, and handler identity
  • Digital files — Use cryptographic hashes or digital signatures to verify files have not been altered

A broken chain of custody can render evidence inadmissible in legal proceedings.

Informing management and law enforcement

Initial incident responders gather logs, monitoring metrics, and user accounts to verify a breach, then immediately notify designated internal leadership and law enforcement authorities as required by organizational policy and applicable law.

Follow the organization's incident response plan — do not destroy evidence or take unauthorized remediation steps before escalation.

Copy of drive — data integrity and preservation

Requires a bit-for-bit (byte-for-byte) clone of the storage drive — ideally using a hardware write blocker — to capture unallocated space and deleted files without altering the original evidence.

Work from the forensic copy for analysis; never modify the original drive. Document hash values of both source and copy to prove integrity.

Incident documentation

Formally records an executive summary, acquisition steps, analytical findings, and definitive conclusions to establish internal clarity and support legal proceedings.

Include timelines, personnel involved, tools used, and all hash values. Thorough documentation protects both the organization and the technician.

Order of volatility

Data must be captured starting with the most volatile components that disappear quickly when power is lost or systems are rebooted. Follow this general sequence:

  1. CPU registers and cache memory
  2. Routing tables, ARP caches, process tables, and RAM
  3. Temporary file systems
  4. Local disk drives
  5. Remote logging and monitoring logs
  6. Network topologies and physical configurations
  7. Archival backup tapes
Capture volatile data first — once a system is powered off or rebooted, RAM contents and running processes are lost permanently.
Part B

Licensing, DRM, and EULAs

Software usage rights, license types, and open-source versus commercial models.

Valid licenses

Software usage must conform to specific licensing models, including:

Per-seat

Assigned to an individual user or device — each installation requires its own license.

Concurrent

Caps simultaneous users — a pool of licenses shared across the organization up to a maximum active count.

Subscription

Duration-based access — rights expire when the subscription period ends unless renewed.

Perpetual license agreement

Grants lifetime access to a software version for a single upfront cost, common in personal-use software. Updates beyond the purchased version may require a separate purchase or maintenance agreement.

Personal-use vs. corporate-use license

Personal license

Covers individual or limited household devices — not valid for business deployment.

Corporate license

Covers enterprise deployments via site licenses or per-seat models, often requiring annual renewals and volume purchase agreements.

Open-source license

Free and Open-Source Software (FOSS) provides full access to the source code for customization and compilation. Closed-source commercial software provides compiled executables only — source code is proprietary.

FOSS still carries license obligations (e.g., GPL copyleft requirements) — "free" does not mean unrestricted use.

Part C

Non-Disclosure Agreements

Legal contracts protecting sensitive commercial information during business relationships.

NDA / MNDA

Non-Disclosure Agreement / Mutual Non-Disclosure Agreement

Legal contracts establishing confidentiality to protect sensitive commercial information and trade secrets.

Unilateral NDA

Binds one party to secrecy — common when a vendor shares proprietary details with a prospective client.

MNDA (bilateral NDA)

Binds both participating organizations — each agrees not to disclose the other's confidential information.

Part D

Regulated data

Sensitive information categories with statutory handling and retention requirements.

Credit card payment information

PCI DSS — Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard

Governs cardholder data with requirements for network isolation, cardholder data protection, access controls, and ongoing auditing.

  • Network segmentation
  • Encryption at rest and in transit
  • Access logging
  • Regular compliance audits

Personal government-issued information

Standardized government identifiers — such as Social Security Numbers or driver's license details — subject to statutory restrictions to prevent identity theft and privacy breaches.

Store minimally, encrypt at rest, and limit access to authorized personnel on a need-to-know basis.

PII — Personally Identifiable Information

Any standalone or combined data point capable of identifying an individual, requiring strict handling policies to prevent unauthorized access or account takeover.

Examples include name, email, phone number, date of birth, or any combination that can uniquely identify a person.

Healthcare data / PHI

Protected Health Information — regulated by HIPAA in the U.S.

Covers patient medical histories, clinical records, and treatment plans. HIPAA mandates administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for any system storing or transmitting PHI.

Data retention requirements

Mandatory storage schedules driven by operational needs — such as versioning or malware recovery — or statutory compliance obligations applying to financial, legal, or government records.

Retention policies define how long data must be kept and when it must be securely destroyed. Exceeding retention periods can create unnecessary legal exposure.

Part E

Policies and compliance

Organizational standards governing acceptable use and regulatory obligations.

Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)

An employee agreement specifying allowed and prohibited uses of organizational IT assets — internet access, hardware, and mobile devices — to limit corporate legal liability.

Users typically acknowledge the AUP during onboarding. Violations can result in disciplinary action or termination.

Regulatory and business compliance requirements

Organizational standards designed to satisfy legal mandates and industry guidelines — spanning data protection, financial reporting, healthcare privacy, and sector-specific regulations.

IT technicians support compliance by enforcing access controls, patch management, audit logging, and secure disposal practices.

Splash screens

Pre-login banner prompts that display operational expectations, legal disclaimers, or liability warnings requiring user consent before access is granted.

Common on corporate workstations and VPN portals — clicking "Accept" constitutes acknowledgment of monitoring and acceptable use terms.
