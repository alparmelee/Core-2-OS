Documentation and Support Systems Information Management

Ticketing systems, asset management, and the documents that keep IT support organized, auditable, and consistent.

Part A

Ticketing systems

Centralized tools for logging, tracking, prioritizing, and resolving support requests.

Ticketing systems

A centralized platform (e.g., ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, Zendesk) used to create, assign, track, and close support incidents and service requests. Tickets provide an audit trail, prevent issues from being lost, and enable reporting on response times and recurring problems.

  • Help desk
  • Service desk
  • Audit trail

User information

Identify who is affected and how to reach them. Standard fields include name, employee ID, department, location, phone number, email, and manager.

Accurate user data ensures callbacks, approvals, and follow-up reach the right person — especially when tickets are reassigned between shifts or tiers.

Device information

Record the hardware or system involved: hostname, asset tag, serial number, model, OS version, IP address, and physical location.

Linking a ticket to a specific device speeds troubleshooting and ties the issue to warranty, configuration, and change history.

Description of issues

The initial report of what the user is experiencing — symptoms, error messages, when it started, and what changed recently.

Capture enough detail to reproduce or diagnose without requiring an immediate callback. Include exact error text, affected applications, and frequency.

Categories

Classify tickets by type so they route to the correct team and appear in meaningful reports. Examples: hardware, software, network, access/permissions, email, printing, mobile, security.

Consistent categorization improves trend analysis — e.g., spotting a spike in VPN tickets after a firewall change.

Severity

Indicates business impact and urgency — how badly operations are affected and how quickly a response is required.

Common levels: Critical (major outage, many users down), High (significant impact, workaround may exist), Medium (limited impact), Low (minor inconvenience, cosmetic).

Severity measures impact. Priority often combines severity with SLA deadlines and may change as context evolves.

Escalation levels

Define when and how a ticket moves to a higher tier of support — Tier 1 (help desk) → Tier 2 (specialists) → Tier 3 (engineers/vendors).

Escalation triggers include SLA breach risk, required expertise, management approval, or vendor involvement. Document the reason and hand off all notes when escalating.

Clear, concise written communication

Ticket notes should be professional, factual, and readable by anyone who picks up the ticket later. Avoid jargon the user may not understand in customer-facing replies; use precise technical terms in internal notes.

Three essential note types:

  • Issue description — What was reported, including symptoms, scope, and environment at the time of the call
  • Progress notes — Chronological log of troubleshooting steps taken, tests run, results, and pending actions
  • Issue resolution — Final fix applied, root cause (if known), verification steps, and any follow-up or preventive measures
Part B

Asset management

Tracking hardware, software, ownership, and lifecycle from purchase through retirement.

Inventory lists

A complete record of organizational assets — desktops, laptops, monitors, printers, mobile devices, servers, and peripherals. Lists typically include make, model, serial number, location, status (in use, spare, retired), and purchase date.

Inventory supports audits, insurance claims, budgeting, and knowing what is deployed before approving new purchases.

Configuration management database (CMDB)

A structured repository that stores configuration items (CIs) and their relationships — not just what you own, but how components connect. A laptop CI might link to its installed OS, software licenses, network segment, and assigned user.

CMDBs support change management, impact analysis, and faster root-cause investigation when a shared dependency fails.

Asset tags and IDs

Unique identifiers (barcode labels, QR codes, or RFID tags) physically affixed to equipment and mirrored in inventory systems.

Asset tags tie a physical device to its digital record for checkout, repair, disposal, and help-desk tickets without relying on serial numbers alone.

Procurement life cycle

The end-to-end process of acquiring IT assets: identify need → request approval → purchase → receive and tag → deploy → maintain → retire/dispose.

Document each stage for budget tracking, compliance, and ensuring retired devices are wiped and disposed of securely.

Warranty and licensing

Warranty — Manufacturer coverage for repair or replacement; track start/end dates and support contract numbers.

Licensing — Legal entitlement to use software (OEM, volume, subscription). Track license keys, seat counts, expiration, and compliance to avoid audits and unexpected true-up costs.

Assigned users

Record which employee, department, or shared resource currently has custody of each asset. Critical for accountability, off-boarding (reclaiming equipment), and locating devices during incidents.

Update assignments promptly when hardware is reissued, pooled, or returned to stock.

Part C

Types of documents

Standardized documentation that supports consistency, compliance, and self-service.

Incident reports

Formal documentation of a security or operational incident: what happened, when, systems affected, actions taken, root cause, and lessons learned.

Used for compliance, management review, and improving future response. More detailed than a closed ticket when regulatory or legal review is required.

Standard operating procedures (SOPs)

Step-by-step instructions for recurring IT tasks so any qualified technician performs them the same way every time.

Example — software package custom installation procedure: Documents prerequisites, silent install switches, post-install configuration, verification steps, and rollback if the deployment fails. Ensures consistent deployments across many machines.

  • Repeatable
  • Version-controlled
  • Training resource

New user / onboarding setup checklist

A ordered list of provisioning tasks for new hires: create AD/Entra account, assign groups, configure email, deploy hardware, install required software, set up MFA, grant building access, and schedule orientation.

Checklists reduce missed steps and speed time-to-productivity. Each item should be checkable with an owner and completion date.

User off-boarding checklist

Security-focused teardown when an employee leaves: disable accounts, revoke licenses, collect hardware, forward email, remove from groups, wipe mobile devices, rotate shared passwords they knew, and archive data per policy.

Off-boarding should be timed with HR — disable access promptly on the last working day or immediately upon termination.

Service-level agreements (SLAs)

Formal commitments defining expected service quality — response time, resolution time, uptime percentage, and support hours.

Internal SLAs

Between IT and other departments inside the organization. Example: help desk responds to P1 tickets within 15 minutes during business hours.

External / third-party SLAs

Between the organization and vendors (ISP, cloud provider, hardware support). Define penalties or credits when uptime or response targets are missed.

Knowledge base / articles

A searchable library of solutions, how-to guides, and FAQs — often linked directly from the ticketing system. Reduces repeat tickets by enabling users and Tier 1 staff to self-resolve common issues.

Good articles include symptoms, cause, step-by-step resolution, screenshots, and the date last verified. Review and update when software or policies change.
