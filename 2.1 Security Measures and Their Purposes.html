2.1

Security Measures and Their Purposes

Physical controls, access methods, biometrics, and logical security frameworks that protect people, facilities, and data.

Physical security Logical security
Physical Bollards CCTV Door locks Access control Biometrics MFA TOTP Zero Trust IAM
Part A

Physical security

Barriers, surveillance, alarms, and personnel that protect a facility and its perimeter.

Bollards

Prevent access — with limits. Channel people through a specific access point while keeping out other things. Allow people; prevent cars and trucks. Identify safety concerns and prevent injuries. Can be used to an extreme: concrete barriers, bollards, even moats.

Access control vestibule

Mantrap

A two-door entry system that controls traffic one person or group at a time.

Normally unlocked

Opening one door causes the others to lock.

Normally locked

Unlocking one door prevents others from being unlocked. When one is open, the other cannot be unlocked.

Badge reader

Magnetic swipe, RFID, or NFC — many different identification methods for different applications.

  • Time clocks
  • Security guard patrols
  • Door access

Video surveillance

CCTV — Closed Circuit Television

Can replace physical guards. Camera features are important — object detection can identify a license plate or a person’s face. Often many different cameras networked together and recorded over time.

  • Motion detection
  • Radio reflection or passive infrared
  • Useful in areas not often in use

Alarm systems

Circuit-based. A circuit is opened or closed — door, window, fence. Useful on the perimeter.

Duress. Triggered by a person — the big red button.

Motion sensors

Motion detection that identifies movement without a camera.

Door locks

  • Conventional — lock and key, deadbolt (physical bolt)
  • Electronic — keyless PIN, token-based, RFID badge, magnetic swipe, or key fob
  • Biometric — hand, finger, or retina
  • Multi-factor — smart card and PIN

Equipment locks

Data center hardware is usually managed by different groups. Responsibility lies with the owner. Racks can be installed side-to-side. Enclosed cabinets with locks — ventilation on front, back, top, and bottom.

Security guards and access lists

Physical protection at the reception area of a facility. Validates identification of existing employees. Provides guest access.

ID badge — picture, name, other details. Must be worn at all times.

Access list — physical list of names, enforced by security guard.

Fences

Build a perimeter — usually very obvious. May not be what you’re looking for.

  • Transparent or opaque
  • Robust — difficult to cut
  • Prevent climbing — razor wire
  • Build it high
Part B

Physical access security

Credentials, biometrics, and detection methods used to verify identity at the door.

Key fobs

Small RFID key added to a physical keychain. Replaces a physical key. Commonly used for door locks — proximity operation and contactless.

Smart cards

Something you have

Certificate-based authentication. Usually requires additional factors. Integrated card reader built into the laptop, or an external USB-connected reader.

Mobile digital key

Replaces physical keys with your phone. Many different apps — automobile unlocking and starting, hotel or office door locks, doors at home. Better authentication than a physical key. Must have access to the phone; unlock for verification.

Keys

Some doors may not have an electronic lock — rarely used. Standalone locks: safe, storage bin, cabinet. Use a key cabinet with formal check-in/check-out and a well-defined storage location. Allows for auditing and timestamps.

Biometrics

Biometric authentication usually stores a mathematical representation of your biometric — your actual fingerprint isn’t usually saved.

Difficult to change. You can change your password. You can’t change your fingerprint. Used in very specific situations — not foolproof.

Retina scanner

Reads the unique capillary structure in the back of the eye.

Fingerprint scanner

Common on phones, laptops, and door access systems.

Palm print scanner

Measures the shape of the hand and fingers.

Facial recognition technology (FRT)

Authenticate using a common biometric feature — your face. Creates a digital “key” from your facial features using a laser dot projector, infrared camera, and 3D facial map.

Used for secure authentication on mobile devices, doors, financial systems, etc. Relatively secure — approximately 1 in 1,000,000 false acceptance rate.

Voice recognition technology

Uses an individual’s voice as a key. The system is “trained” by speaking phrases; results are evaluated and stored. Verify yourself with a different phrase — voice recognition software listens for the original speaker.

Questionable security on its own. May be best combined with other factors. You may not always be able to speak.

Lighting

More light means more security. Attacks avoid the light. Easier to see when lit. Non-IR cameras can see better.

Specialized design — consider overall light levels. Lighting angles may be important for facial recognition. Avoid shadows and glare.

Magnetometers

Passive scanning that detects metal objects. Not useful for non-metal objects — won’t identify ceramic or plastic.

Part C

Logical security

Policies, authentication, access control, and identity frameworks that protect digital resources.

Principle of least privilege

Rights and permissions should be set to the bare minimum — you only get what’s needed to complete your objective.

  • All user accounts must be limited
  • Applications should run with minimal privileges
  • Don’t allow users to run with admin privileges
Limits the scope of malicious behavior.

Zero Trust model

Many networks are relatively open on the inside — once you’re through the firewall, there are few security controls. Zero Trust is a holistic approach to network security covering every device, every process, and every person.

Everything must be verified. Nothing is inherently trusted.

  • Multi-factor authentication
  • Encryption
  • System permissions
  • Additional firewalls
  • Monitoring and analytics

Access control lists (ACLs)

Used to allow or deny traffic. Also used for NAT, QoS, etc. Commonly applied on the ingress or egress of a router interface.

ACLs evaluate certain criteria: source IP, destination IP, TCP port numbers, UDP port numbers, ICMP — then deny or permit. What happens when an ACL matches the traffic?

Multifactor authentication (MFA)

Prove who you are using different methods — a memorized password, a mobile app, your GPS location.

  • Something you know
  • Something you have
  • Something you are
  • Somewhere you are

Email auth

Associate a person with an email address. Validate a person at that email — useful during registration. Verify a user’s email address. Use instead of a password (username is your email).

Can be helpful later for password resets and validation code delivery.

Hardware token

A physical security device that generates or stores authentication codes to verify a user’s identity when logging into an account or system.

Authenticator application

Pseudo-random token generators — a useful authentication factor. Carry a physical hardware token generator, or use a software-based token generator on your phone. Powerful and convenient.

Short Message Service (SMS)

Text messaging — includes more than text these days. A login factor can be sent via SMS to a predefined phone number: provide username and password, phone receives an SMS, input the code into the login form.

Security issues exist: phone number can be reassigned, SMS messages can be intercepted, SMS spoofing is possible.

Voice call

A phone call provides the token — the computer is talking to you. Similar disadvantages to SMS: phone can be intercepted or forwarded, phone number can be added to another phone.

Time-based one-time password (TOTP)

Uses a secret key and the time of day — no incremental counter. Secret key is configured ahead of time. Timestamps are synchronized via NTP and usually increment every 30 seconds.

Enter username, password, and TOTP code. One of the most common OTP methods — used by Google, Facebook, Microsoft, etc.

One-time password / passcode (OTP)

Use them once and never again — once per session, once per authentication attempt. Keys are based on a secret key and a counter. Token-based authentication: the hash is different every time. Hardware and software tokens available.

Security Assertions Markup Language (SAML)

An open standard that lets security systems securely share your identity details so you don’t have to re-enter credentials across different websites.

Single sign-on (SSO)

An authentication feature that lets you log in just once to access all your connected apps and services seamlessly.

Just-in-time access

A security practice that grants elevated user permissions only for the exact amount of time needed to complete a task, then revokes them automatically.

Privileged access management (PAM)

Tools and policies designed to lock down, monitor, and audit superuser or admin accounts with high-level access.

Mobile device management (MDM)

IT software that allows administrators to remotely configure, monitor, and secure smartphones and laptops used within an organization.

Data loss prevention (DLP)

Security software that detects and blocks sensitive or confidential information from leaving the organization’s network.

Identity access management (IAM)

The overarching framework of policies and technologies that controls who has access to which digital resources.

Directory services

A centralized network database that stores, organizes, and manages user accounts, devices, and permissions across an entire organization.
