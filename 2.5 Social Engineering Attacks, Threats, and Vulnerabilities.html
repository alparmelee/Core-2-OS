Social Engineering Attacks, Threats, and Vulnerabilities

How attackers manipulate people, exploit systems, and take advantage of weak security posture.

Social engineering Threats
Phishing Vishing Spear phishing Tailgating DoS DDoS Zero-day SQLi XSS Vulnerabilities
Part A

Social engineering attacks

Manipulating people into revealing information, granting access, or taking unsafe actions.

Phishing

Generic fraudulent emails sent to mass audiences attempting to trick victims into revealing sensitive credentials or clicking malicious links.

Vishing

Voice phishing

Voice-based phishing attacks where scammers use phone calls to manipulate victims into providing confidential information.

Smishing

SMS phishing

Phishing attacks delivered via SMS text messages containing malicious links or urgent requests for personal data.

QR code phishing (quishing)

Malicious QR codes placed in physical or digital locations that direct users to credential-harvesting websites when scanned.

Spear phishing

Highly targeted phishing campaigns tailored to a specific individual or organization using gathered personal details.

Whaling

CEO fraud — targets big fish

A specialized form of spear phishing that specifically targets high-profile executives, business owners, or senior leadership.

Shoulder surfing

Observing a target’s screen or keyboard directly — either in person or through cameras — to steal sensitive data or passwords.

Tailgating

Piggybacking

Following an authorized person through a restricted physical door or entryway without validating credentials.

Impersonation

Pretending to be a trusted figure, such as an IT technician or executive, to trick employees into granting access or resetting passwords.

Dumpster diving

Searching through discarded trash, recycling bins, or paper shredder waste to recover sensitive documents or configuration details.

Part B

Threats

Attack types and techniques that compromise availability, confidentiality, or integrity.

Denial of Service (DoS)

Overwhelms a target system or service with traffic from a single source to disrupt availability.

Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS)

Floods a target system using multiple compromised systems (botnets) from distributed locations to crash services.

DoS

Single source overwhelms the target.

DDoS

Many distributed sources — harder to block.

Evil twin

A rogue Wi-Fi access point set up to mimic a legitimate network to intercept user traffic and credentials.

Zero-day attack

An exploit targeting a previously unknown software vulnerability before the vendor releases a patch.

No patch exists yet — defenders have zero days to prepare.

Spoofing

Falsifying network headers, IP addresses, or sender identities to disguise the origin of malicious traffic or communications.

On-path attack

Formerly man-in-the-middle (MITM)

Secretly relaying and altering communications between two parties who believe they are directly communicating with each other.

Brute-force attack

Systematically testing every possible character combination until the correct password or key is found.

Dictionary attack

Automated password cracking using a predefined list of common words, phrases, and leaked passwords.

vs brute-force: Dictionary attacks try likely passwords first — faster but less exhaustive.

Insider threat

Security risks originating from current or former employees, contractors, or business partners with authorized access.

SQL injection (SQLi)

Injecting malicious database queries into application entry fields to read or manipulate backend database contents.

Cross-site scripting (XSS)

Injecting malicious client-side scripts into trusted web applications to execute in victim browsers and steal session tokens.

Business email compromise (BEC)

Hijacking or spoofing executive email accounts to trick finance teams or employees into executing unauthorized wire transfers.

Supply chain attack

Compromising a third-party vendor, software dependency, or service provider to breach target organizations downstream.

Part C

Vulnerabilities

Weaknesses in systems, policies, and device management that attackers exploit.

Non-compliant systems

Devices or systems that violate organizational security policies, baseline standards, or regulatory frameworks.

Unpatched systems

Devices running software with known security flaws due to missing vendor updates and security fixes.

Unprotected systems

Systems lacking basic baseline security controls, such as active antivirus software or properly configured local firewalls.

End-of-life (EOL)

Hardware or software no longer supported or patched by vendors, leaving newly discovered vulnerabilities permanently unaddressed.

Bring Your Own Device (BYOD)

Unmanaged personal employee devices connected to enterprise resources without strict organizational security enforcement.
