Part A

Application integration

How AI is embedded into tools IT professionals and end users interact with daily.

AI in everyday tools

Everyday tools routinely feature integrated AI, including:

  • Search engines — Offering aggregated query summaries instead of only ranked link lists
  • Email clients — Generating quick message overviews and suggested replies
  • Graphics editors — Utilizing generative fill to insert or remove image elements seamlessly

IT support staff should understand these integrations so they can troubleshoot user issues and advise on organizational policy compliance.

Part B

Policy

Appropriate and inappropriate uses of AI in professional and educational contexts.

Appropriate use

Legitimate AI applications in IT and enterprise environments include:

  • Parsing enterprise log files to detect security threats
  • Making automated workflow decisions based on defined rules
  • Evaluating healthcare imaging (e.g., MRI scans) to assist clinicians
  • Checking prescriptions for harmful drug interactions
  • Providing real-time language translations for global support teams

Appropriate use aligns with organizational policy, respects data privacy, and keeps a human in the loop for critical decisions.

Improper use and plagiarism

Inappropriate applications include:

  • Generating deepfakes for fraud or impersonation
  • Executing unvetted AI-generated code in production environments
  • Claiming ownership of AI-created imagery or content without disclosure
  • Submitting AI-synthesized summaries for school or college assignments without citing the source
Never paste confidential company data, credentials, or customer PII into public AI tools — inputs may be stored or used for model training.
Part C

Limitations

Known weaknesses that make AI unsuitable as a sole authority without human verification.

Bias

Occurs when training data sets are incomplete or unrepresentative, causing the model to yield unfair or skewed outcomes.

Examples include underrepresented demographics in medical data leading to misdiagnosis risk, or biased resume keyword preferences that disadvantage qualified candidates.

Hallucinations

Instances where AI confidently presents false information — such as misidentifying images or providing incorrect historical facts during product announcements.

Hallucinations look authoritative but are fabricated. Always verify AI output against trusted primary sources before acting on it.

Accuracy

Current models frequently produce inaccurate predictions or incorrect factual statements during evaluation tests, demonstrating that AI tools remain far from error-free.

Treat AI as an assistant, not an oracle — especially for technical troubleshooting steps, legal advice, or security recommendations.

Part D

Private vs. public AI

Understanding where data goes and who controls the model when choosing an AI platform.

Public AI

Internet-accessible tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Google Gemini) trained on vast internet-wide data sources. They offer broad informational access but pose data privacy and security risks if sensitive details or personal profiles are ingested.

Inputs may be logged, reviewed, or used to improve future models unless the provider offers an enterprise tier with data isolation guarantees.

Private AI

Enterprise-controlled environments operated internally. They restrict access to proprietary organizational data, enhancing data security and protecting internal secrets from external exposure.

Public AI

Broad knowledge, easy access, privacy risk for sensitive data.

Private AI

Data stays on-premises or in a controlled cloud tenant; higher setup cost, stronger compliance.
